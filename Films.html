The Nightmare Before Christmas is so visually captivating that I want to watch it again and again, just to take in every detail of how its characters and settings are designed and brought to life. There is so much imagination behind it, and the story makes it all the more enjoyable.

We follow Jack, the Pumpkin King of Halloween Town, as he goes through an existential crisis and longs for something different. Then there is Sally. She wants to experience more of the world but is trapped in her creator’s home. Both Jack and Sally long for new experiences, and I think that shared desire connects them.

I’m not sure whether I was simply too enchanted by the film’s creativity, but I didn’t connect with the characters as deeply as I expected to. Perhaps on another viewing, I’ll find myself more drawn to their stories.

I feel lucky to have made it this far without having The Usual Suspects spoiled for me. Knowing the twist beforehand would take so much away from the experience. Even so, I guessed the ending very early on.

As the film built up the mystery surrounding Keyser Söze, the camera’s attention to his walk made me think of Verbal Kint’s distinctive limp. I started to suspect that Verbal was faking his disability and might be making up the story too.

By the end, I found myself thinking of Andy’s excuse in the SNL sketch. Since I hadn’t seen The Usual Suspects before, I’d never realized that the sketch was referencing this film.

At the beginning of Corpse Bride, I thought the film would focus on Victor and Victoria’s story. Both have strict parents who care little about what their children want, especially when it comes to marriage. They treat marriage as a business transaction, which is hardly surprising given the film’s Victorian setting. Victor and Victoria are both nervous, each carrying their own fears about marrying someone they barely know. Will they love each other? Will they be happy?

But their relationship is not the heart of the film. There is also Emily, the corpse bride, who continues to carry the dreams she had before she died. Even in death, she feels the pain of those unfulfilled dreams, a longing that survives in a body without a beating heart.

Toward the end of the film, Victor decides to stay in the world of the dead. Emily is finally close to having the life she always dreamed of, yet she realizes that she needs to let it go. What makes this moment so moving is that the choice is hers. She does not simply step aside because Victor sees Victoria. She could tell herself that Victor belongs to her, that she deserves him after everything she has suffered. She could let him drink the poison so they could remain together in the world of the dead. Instead, she lets him go, freeing herself from her longing for the life that was taken from her.

Emily’s story is heartbreaking. The person she was going to marry stole her life and her chance to experience everything she had hoped for. She carries that loss even beyond death. When she finally releases her unfinished dreams and turns into butterflies, it feels like a moment of freedom. Perhaps there are times when peace comes from accepting what can no longer be. The dreams we hold on to can also become a trap that keeps us from moving forward.

Oldboy was the most disturbing film I’ve ever watched.

I’m not even sure whether there was supposed to be some deeper meaning behind it. And even if there was, I honestly can’t imagine what it might be. To me, it felt as though the filmmakers simply set out to make the most disturbing film they possibly could.

In Inception, we’re introduced to a reality where dreams exist within dreams, and people can enter, control, and shape them however they want. It’s essentially a heavily distorted version of our own reality.

After watching the film, I did a little searching on Reddit and came across so many discussions about what certain scenes actually meant, whether something was real, and what the filmmakers were trying to suggest. Personally, what I appreciated most was the film’s originality. I found the questions it raised about ultimate reality and how ideas shape our perception of reality fascinating.

But I don’t necessarily want to turn the experience into an endless search for hidden meanings or definitive answers. Maybe I’ll watch it again someday, and if I do, I’ll simply take away whatever I happen to see in it that time.

Even though it was almost three hours long, I completely lost track of time while watching it. In fact, I didn’t even know the film was three hours long when I went to the theater. I only realized it afterward, when a friend mentioned its runtime while we were talking about it. The fact that it managed to hold my attention for three whole hours is an achievement in itself.

When I first came across this project, I thought they were making a film about Elizabeth Holmes. I didn’t know A24 was involved either. But later, when I found out that it wasn’t a film but a documentary produced by A24, I became interested. Actually, I decided to watch it not so much because I was interested in Elizabeth Holmes’s story but because I was curious to see what kind of production A24 had put together. There are also plenty of people who were already familiar with Nathan Fielder and watched it specifically because of him, but this was my first introduction to his work.

Since it was described as a documentary, I naturally expected something fairly informative, with an attempt to uncover the truth. But I couldn’t have been more wrong.

We get a glimpse into Elizabeth Holmes’s life during the month or so before she goes to prison. Fielder and his crew spend this time with Holmes and her family, and we experience these moments through their cameras. Once Holmes goes to prison, their contact with her becomes more limited, and we watch the moments they couldn’t capture on camera through reenactments performed by Amanda Seyfried. In these scenes, we don’t just see what happened. We also get a sense of what Fielder heard in those moments, how he interpreted what he saw, and how all of it made him feel.

I want to come back to Amanda Seyfried’s involvement in the documentary because it’s another aspect of the film that I particularly enjoyed. This isn’t Seyfried’s first time portraying Elizabeth Holmes. She previously played her in The Dropout, a series about Holmes’s story. Early in the documentary, Holmes mentions that she doesn’t think The Dropout accurately represents who she is and that it portrays her as a different person from who she really is. Then, about halfway through the documentary, Seyfried enters the picture, this time as the actress who has already portrayed Holmes. We get to watch her reactions to the conversations between Holmes and Fielder, from the perspective of someone who has previously played Holmes on screen. She hadn’t had a chance to speak with Elizabeth at the time, so she created her own version of Holmes based on the information available to her. I think this adds another interesting layer to the documentary, as we get to see the distance between the person Holmes presents herself to be, the version of her that Seyfried created, and the way others perceive her.

The documentary doesn’t try to draw us into a debate about whether Elizabeth Holmes is actually guilty or innocent. Instead, it made me reflect on just how abstract and subjective reality can be. And by reality, I don’t mean whether Holmes is guilty or innocent. I’m referring more to the way Fielder perceives the moments he experiences, the meanings he draws from what he sees and hears, and how his feelings become a kind of reality of their own. And for me, the most fascinating aspect of the documentary was that it made me realize just how complicated it can be to perceive reality and communicate it to others.

I watched The House after seeing it recommended by gunsfilms. She made a post saying, “If you liked Coraline, you might like these too.”

My introduction to Coraline, though, was completely accidental. I had seen a few things about the film before, but it had never really caught my attention. I just didn’t think it would be something I’d particularly enjoy.

A few months ago, I was out for a walk, as I often am. Whenever I come across a Little Free Library on my walks, I always make sure to take a look. That day, I happened to find a copy of Coraline. I had no idea the film was based on a book. I took it home and started reading it right away. After getting a little way into the book, my husband and I thought, why not watch Coraline that evening? I finished the book the next day. I really enjoyed both the story and the way it was told.

Anyway, Coraline isn’t what we’re here to talk about. I just wanted to share a little about how I ended up watching The House.

So, back to The House. I really liked the film’s unsettling atmosphere and the music that built up the tension. It felt like the perfect film for October. I also appreciated its critique of materialism. Still, I feel like the film was a little too heavy on symbolism. I’m not entirely sure how much of it I picked up on and how much I missed.

I’ve always loved stories centered around a single house, where we get to see the different lives unfolding within it.

But I think what really made the film stand out to me was the beauty of its stop-motion animation. And, of course, the fact that I ended up watching it because I loved Coraline.

The film is so much more than what the trailer leads you to expect. I actually think it was a great decision not to give too much away, because watching everything unfold without knowing what you’re really looking at, and then experiencing those moments of realization, is an essential part of the film itself.

The cinematography captures these feelings beautifully. At first, the film has a colder atmosphere, somewhat reminiscent of Yorgos Lanthimos’s films. Seeing Digger Rockwell (Tom Cruise) in this setting, wearing a cowboy hat and boots and speaking with a strange Southern accent, made me wonder, "What kind of film are we watching? Where is all of this going?" But as the story unfolds, we begin to understand how the scenes that initially seemed disconnected fit into the larger narrative. Especially in the final moments, as the peculiar world in which the story takes place becomes more clearly defined, the colors, visuals, and details all come together to form a cohesive whole.

I’m trying to write this without giving away any spoilers because I believe that the less you know about the film going in, the more you’ll enjoy it. I imagine that’s also why the trailer makes it look like an entirely different kind of film. Unfortunately, keeping the film’s most intriguing aspects hidden may have contributed to its failure to attract a larger audience and have a strong opening.

Although the world depicted on screen looks very different from our own, the emotions and experiences it evokes feel incredibly true to life. I think a large part of this comes from the film’s refusal to portray some people as entirely responsible for everything while presenting others as nothing more than victims. What I mean isn’t that everyone is bad or that everyone shares equal responsibility. Rather, it’s about showing where society stands in relation to what’s happening and how people choose to respond to it. To me, this reflects the world we’re living in today remarkably well.

I’m going to talk about my favorite scene next. I don’t think it’s a major spoiler, but if you’d rather go into the film knowing as little as possible, I’d recommend stopping here.

One of my favorite moments in the entire film comes during Digger’s trial. After his final monologue, the crowd completely forgets about him and turns against one another. I’m so glad they chose to write it this way.

A lot of the criticism I’ve seen about the film revolves around who the real villain is, or whether the film is trying to make us sympathize with predators. But I didn’t feel that the film was trying to create a villain or encourage us to sympathize with predators and see them as victims. I don’t think showing the human side of a character necessarily means justifying or excusing their actions. In fact, one of my favorite things about the film was how it encouraged us to look beyond simply labeling characters as good or bad.

I think one of the reasons people see Chris Hansen (Robert Pattinson) as the villain is that he comes across as a bit of a psychopath. But Hansen is going through one of the most stressful periods of both his professional and personal life. To me, it seems natural that someone in that situation would experience more intense emotional ups and downs. When the difficulties in his personal life collide with the pressures of his work, I find it hard to judge his emotional breakdowns without considering the circumstances he’s in. So, watching him in those moments didn’t exactly make me think, "Wow, Chris Hansen is a complete psychopath." If anything, he felt more human to me. What I saw was someone struggling to deal with his emotions.

I also think Hansen is someone who experiences emotions intensely and is deeply affected by what others go through. The way he internalizes the story set in India, and the fact that he, like the little girl, begins seeing monsters, only reinforced this impression. Of course, none of this justifies everything he does, but it allows us to understand the character not only through his actions but also through his feelings.

Of course, To Catch a Predator is a highly controversial show when it comes to media ethics. As the film itself shows, the producers’ motivations are largely driven by the desire to create successful entertainment. On the one hand, the show can be seen as serving a positive purpose by catching offenders and protecting children from them. On the other hand, turning that purpose into a form of entertainment makes the whole issue much more complicated. The fact that someone dies by suicide during the course of the show, and that the incident is broadcast, raises even more ethical questions about the methods being used.

For me, the important point is that the show’s potential to produce positive outcomes doesn’t automatically justify every method used to achieve them. At the same time, the fact that the show raises serious ethical concerns doesn’t necessarily make Hansen an entirely ill-intentioned person. I found it much more interesting that, rather than trying to identify a real villain, I found it much more interesting to see the contradictions between people’s intentions, their actions, and the circumstances they find themselves in.
